# The Attendant: Pinewood Mall — Story Bible

**Status:** Canonical narrative source of truth for the expanded game  
**Scope:** Characters, history, six-chapter arc, reveals, evidence, endings, supernatural rules, environmental storytelling  
**Precedence:** The user's newest explicit direction overrides this document. Otherwise, new narrative work must remain consistent with this bible.

---

# 1. The promise of Pinewood

**The Attendant: Pinewood Mall** is a first-person horror game about an ordinary maintenance contractor entering an abandoned shopping mall for a routine after-hours service call and discovering that the building has been trying to finish the same closing shift since 1997.

Pinewood's horror should come from three things colliding:

1. **A believable dead mall.** Stores, employee spaces, service infrastructure, obsolete technology, stains, records, forgotten personal possessions, cheap corporate signage and the remains of ordinary lives.
2. **Corporate procedure made supernatural.** Work orders, employee rosters, closing checklists, security states, key control, time clocks and automated announcements continue to behave as though procedure matters more than human life.
3. **The Attendant.** A humanoid enforcement presence that appears when Pinewood calls for staff who are not where the building thinks they should be.

The player is never an action hero. Pinewood is survived through observation, maintenance knowledge, environmental problem-solving, hiding, route planning, sound management, distraction and eventually understanding the building's rules well enough to finish what was left incomplete.

The central thematic sentence is:

> **Pinewood cannot close because, according to Pinewood, someone is still working.**

The true ending is not killing The Attendant. It is making that sentence false.

---

# 2. Tone and narrative principles

## 2.1 Horror should accumulate

The game should not begin at maximum supernatural intensity. Chapter 1 must allow the player to believe, for a while, that Pinewood is merely unsafe, neglected and technologically broken. Each chapter removes one mundane explanation.

The progression is:

- Something is wrong with the mall.
- Something is wrong with the work order.
- Something is wrong with the history.
- Something is wrong with the radio.
- Something is wrong with the building's memory.
- Something is wrong with the player's own status inside the system.

## 2.2 The lore must be physical

No critical revelation should exist in only one optional note.

Important facts should be delivered through at least two channels chosen from:

- radio dialogue,
- PA announcements,
- environmental set pieces,
- physical evidence,
- photographs,
- employee belongings,
- security video,
- audio recordings,
- computer/terminal records,
- bodies or remains,
- architecture,
- objective consequences.

A player who explores deeply should understand more, but a player who misses optional lore must still understand the main plot.

## 2.3 Pinewood was a real workplace

The 1997 staff are not disposable horror victims. They had jobs, jokes, schedules, irritations, relationships and plans for the weekend. Their humanity must be visible before or alongside their fates.

A staff photograph should make a later name recognizable. A locker should tell us who owned it before a body does. A handwritten argument in a maintenance log can matter as much as a supernatural recording.

## 2.4 Bodies are punctuation, not wallpaper

Human remains should be rare enough that finding one changes the emotional temperature of the game.

The main campaign should contain three major body/remains discoveries:

1. **Gavin Cole**, a recent victim, proving Pinewood is still drawing people in.
2. **Luis Ortega**, a 1997 victim, physically validating the old records and revealing what Security learned.
3. **Eli Mercer**, the missing contractor at the center of the closing fault.

Other deaths can be implied without scattering corpses everywhere.

## 2.5 Do not over-explain The Attendant

The game may explain what causes The Attendant to appear and what rules govern it.

The game must **not** explain what species/entity/afterlife category it belongs to, where it originally came from, or provide a neat occult mythology.

Pinewood is more frightening if the human systems are understandable and the thing complying with them is not.

# 3. Canon truths

These are facts the development team should treat as true even when the player never receives definitive proof.

1. The present-day Dispatcher, **Renee Ward**, is a real living person outside Pinewood.
2. The original emergency maintenance work order was created on **November 14, 1997**.
3. The current player is not the first contractor to receive a descendant of that work order.
4. “Contractor Fourteen” is not merely a random call sign. Pinewood's legacy system has created **fourteen contractor accountability sessions** tied to the unresolved work order since 1997. Not all fourteen resulted in a person entering the building.
5. **Eli Mercer**, an outside electrical contractor, entered Pinewood during the 1997 incident and never successfully clocked out of the mall's accountability system.
6. Eli is dead. His physical remains are still inside Pinewood.
7. Eli is **not** The Attendant.
8. The PA intelligence and The Attendant are **not the same entity**.
9. Pinewood's closing/accountability software did not consciously become evil. It is a rigid human-built system continuing to insist that an unresolved condition be corrected.
10. The Attendant becomes active when Pinewood's closing system generates staff-location/accountability calls.
11. The closing software did not create The Attendant from nothing. Evidence exists of a similar figure or “night employee” in Pinewood records predating the 1997 accountability upgrade.
12. It is intentionally unknown whether The Attendant is a preexisting phenomenon that learned to obey Pinewood, a manifestation shaped by years of routine, or something stranger.
13. Pinewood's supernatural activity can influence old and modern electronic systems connected to its unresolved service/accountability records. The exact mechanism is never fully explained.
14. Pinewood can imitate voices it has repeatedly heard through its own PA, radio and telephone infrastructure. At first the imitation is imperfect. It improves.
15. The true ending permanently resolves the 1997 closing fault. The normal ending suppresses or bypasses it but leaves the underlying loop intact.

# 4. Pinewood Mall history

## 1979 — Opening

Pinewood Mall opens as a mid-sized regional shopping center. The original building has a central fountain, two anchor spaces, an east and west concourse, service corridors behind the tenant walls and a below-grade loading/service level.

Early employee folklore mentions a “night man” or “closing man” seen in distant corridors after security sweeps. These stories are inconsistent, mundane-sounding and mostly treated as jokes. No clear evidence connects them to later events.

## 1988–1994 — Expansion and modernization

Pinewood adds new retail frontage, remodels the food court and installs increasingly centralized security, paging and building-control systems. Old wiring and new systems coexist badly.

## 1996 — Decline hidden by renovation

Pinewood is already beginning to struggle financially, but management spends heavily on cosmetic modernization and operational automation to reduce overnight staffing costs.

Mall operations director **Martin Kessler** becomes the internal champion of a computerized closing/accountability project.

## 1997 — PCAS installation

Pinewood installs the **Pinewood Closing & Accountability System (PCAS)**.

PCAS is not futuristic AI. It is plausible late-1990s facility software tying together:

- employee schedule imports,
- badge/time-clock events,
- service-key checkout,
- selected security zones,
- loading/service-door state,
- PA closing announcements,
- emergency maintenance tickets,
- after-hours contractor temporary sessions.

The overnight security state is supposed to begin only when all scheduled or registered workers have cleared the building.

The critical variable is effectively:

> **ACCOUNTABILITY = 0**

If anyone remains registered inside, PCAS maintains a closing hold and repeatedly calls for unresolved staff.

Management requests that the automated attendance/accountability feature not be advertised to tenants because employees dislike the feeling of being tracked. This becomes important later when investigators cannot easily understand what the system was doing.

## November 14, 1997 — The Last Shift

The defining incident occurs. See the detailed timeline below.

## 1998–2005 — The cover-up years

Pinewood reopens after a short “electrical and fire-safety closure.” The service-level rooms most affected by the incident are sealed or reclassified.

Martin Kessler and ownership's attorneys characterize the night as an electrical failure complicated by staff panic. Eli Mercer is listed as having left the property even though no valid exit event exists. Luis Ortega's disappearance is treated as voluntary abandonment after evidence suggests he left his security post.

Jo Alvarez disputes the official account and is terminated after refusing to sign a revised incident statement.

The mall repeatedly experiences strange closing events:

- pages after the PA amplifier has been shut down,
- doors refusing to release until employee counts are cleared,
- cameras showing a dark person-like figure after staff calls,
- service tickets reopening themselves,
- employee time records containing impossible duplicate entries.

Management uses manual overrides rather than investigating the root condition.

## 2006–2019 — Long decline

Tenant turnover accelerates. Parts of the east wing close. Overnight staffing is reduced.

Contractors occasionally report impossible work requests attached to Pinewood. Some are cancelled before anyone arrives. A few generate incident reports. Those become part of the hidden “Contractor 01–13” history.

The mall's reputation becomes a mixture of ordinary dead-mall gossip and employee folklore.

## 2020 — Public closure

Pinewood Mall closes to shoppers.

The property remains powered at a minimal level for alarms, environmental systems, ownership inspections and redevelopment evaluation. PCAS remains connected to legacy building systems because nobody fully understands what can safely replace it.

## 2023 — Gavin Cole

Property inspector **Gavin Cole** enters Pinewood after receiving a facilities ticket while surveying the building for a potential sale.

He never completes his inspection.

His disappearance is not publicly connected to Pinewood. His body remains below grade and becomes the first major corpse found by the player.

## Present day

A legacy property-management migration exposes Pinewood's old maintenance queue to a modern vendor dispatch network.

Renee Ward's dispatch system receives an emergency electrical ticket from Pinewood.

The ticket looks current.

Its original creation record is from 1997.

The player accepts the job.

PCAS registers **CONTRACTOR 14**.

# 5. The 1997 cast

## Eli Mercer — outside electrical contractor

**Age in 1997:** 34  
**Role:** Emergency electrical contractor  
**Fate:** Dies inside Pinewood; remains unresolved in PCAS until the true ending

Eli is competent, practical and initially irritated by the call. He has no supernatural knowledge. His maintenance recorder begins as ordinary job documentation and becomes increasingly personal as the night collapses.

He is the historical mirror of the player, but not a perfect duplicate. Eli makes understandable mistakes the player can learn from.

Most important: **Eli is not The Attendant.** The story should deliberately tempt the player toward that conclusion before disproving it.

Eli reaches deeper into Pinewood than management realizes. His final evidence shows that he understood the accountability problem and attempted to reach PA control, but was injured before he could complete the closing sequence.

## Jo Alvarez — closing manager

**Age in 1997:** 41  
**Role:** Mall closing manager  
**Fate:** Survives 1997; fired in 1998 after challenging the official report

Jo is the human moral center of the 1997 story. She cares more about getting people out than protecting Pinewood's systems or management's reputation.

She notices the closing count is wrong before anyone else. She personally verifies staff departures and starts handwriting names because she no longer trusts the terminal.

Her handwritten roster is ultimately the basis for the true ending.

Jo gets Tessa Kim out of the building through receiving during the chaos. She later gives a statement contradicting Martin Kessler's account. The statement is buried in property litigation records and rediscovered by Renee in the present day.

## Luis Ortega — night security supervisor

**Age in 1997:** 46  
**Role:** Night security supervisor  
**Fate:** Dies inside a secondary security room

Luis is skeptical of employee ghost stories but methodical about cameras and timestamps.

He is the first person to identify a repeatable relationship:

1. PCAS calls for an unresolved worker.
2. cameras glitch,
3. a dark humanoid appears on a route leading toward the called location.

Luis attempts to disable automatic staff paging. The PA continues after he physically removes power from part of the announcement chain.

He barricades himself in secondary Security after trying to lead The Attendant away from remaining staff. His remains and final VHS/security evidence are found in Chapter 3.

## Tessa Kim — Cassette Castle employee

**Age in 1997:** 19  
**Role:** Closing clerk at Cassette Castle  
**Fate:** Survives the Last Shift

Tessa gives Pinewood an ordinary civilian life that predates the horror. She likes making mixtapes, hates the mall's new attendance system and intends to leave the job after the holidays.

Her locker, staff photograph, handwritten “Top 40,” personal cassette and closing checklist make her recognizable long before the player learns what happened to her.

During the incident PCAS repeatedly reports Tessa as unresolved even after she has clocked out, making her one of The Attendant's early search targets. Jo gets her outside manually.

This proves an important point: **PCAS records can be wrong.** The supernatural loop cares about what the system believes, not necessarily physical truth.

## Andre Bell — Pinewood maintenance technician

**Age in 1997:** 52  
**Role:** Building maintenance technician  
**Fate:** Survives with injuries; resigns immediately

Andre knows the building's old electrical infrastructure better than anyone. He is the person who tells Eli that parts of Pinewood's service network predate the new computer system.

He disconnects the fountain motor during the incident because it begins cycling despite a dead branch circuit. His maintenance tag becomes LS-01.

Andre escapes through the loading dock after being injured by a closing shutter. His later written statement contains the earliest reference to older staff stories about “the man who comes when closing calls.”

## Martin Kessler — mall operations director

**Age in 1997:** 48  
**Role:** Pinewood operations director  
**Fate:** Survives; helps suppress the incident

Kessler is not a cultist and does not understand the supernatural truth.

His sin is much more ordinary: liability management.

He authorized PCAS, ignored employee complaints, pushed manual overrides after the incident and prioritized reopening the mall over determining what happened to Eli and Luis.

Kessler falsifies or approves inaccurate incident records indicating that all outside contractors cleared the building.

The cover-up does not create Pinewood's horror. It prevents anyone from resolving it.

## Additional closing staff

The true ending roster includes several employees who are less central but still humanized through small artifacts:

- **Denise Park**, Video Planet assistant manager, survived.
- **Marcus Reed**, food-court custodian, survived.
- **Nina Flores**, Sunburst Arcade closer, survived.
- **Caleb Moss**, loading associate, survived.

These names provide texture, roster reconstruction and environmental stories without requiring every person to become a major subplot.

# 6. November 14, 1997 — The Last Shift timeline

Exact minute values may be adjusted during implementation for pacing, but event order is canon.

## 8:55 PM — Normal closing begins

The public closing announcement plays. Storefronts begin shutting down. PCAS begins its first live full-mall accountability cycle.

## 9:03 PM — Electrical instability

A feeder fault causes lights to sag across the east concourse and service level. Several PCAS terminals lose communication and reconnect.

Badge/time-clock events begin duplicating or failing.

## 9:08 PM — First accountability mismatch

PCAS shows at least two employees still inside who Jo can physically see leaving.

Jo starts a handwritten roster.

## 9:12 PM — Andre disconnects the fountain motor

The fountain pump begins cycling despite the affected branch being isolated. Andre disconnects it locally.

Minutes later, staff report hearing running water near the dry fountain.

## 9:18 PM — Emergency contractor ticket generated

PCAS creates an after-hours electrical work order. The temporary contractor field is opened but remains unresolved.

## 9:26 PM — Eli Mercer arrives

Eli signs into Pinewood through the service entrance and receives a temporary contractor badge/key authorization.

PCAS adds him to accountability.

## 9:31 PM — The first impossible staff page

The PA calls for an employee who has already left the property.

Luis sees an indistinct humanoid on a camera several corridors from the called location.

## 9:37 PM — Tessa is called

PCAS announces Tessa Kim as unresolved even though her time clock shows an exit event.

Tessa and Jo hear footsteps outside Cassette Castle while Luis sees the dark figure moving toward that side of the mall.

Jo takes Tessa toward receiving instead of following PCAS instructions.

## 9:44 PM — PA power interruption

Luis and Andre disconnect part of the PA playback equipment.

The next closing announcement still occurs.

The voice is cleaner than the damaged speakers should allow.

## 9:49 PM — First direct encounter

Luis encounters The Attendant at a distance in a service corridor. His radio transmission describes it as employee-shaped but visually wrong, with bright eyes and no readable face.

Eli initially assumes Luis is seeing a trespasser.

## 9:54 PM — Jo gets Tessa outside

Jo uses receiving rather than an approved staff exit. PCAS does not record the departure correctly.

This deepens the accountability fault.

## 10:02 PM — Closing hold escalates

PCAS refuses overnight transition. Automatic announcements increase. Several service doors change state unexpectedly.

The Attendant becomes more active after each unresolved-staff page.

## 10:11 PM — Kessler orders an override

From off-site, Martin Kessler instructs staff to use a management override rather than evacuating the property and calling emergency services. He is concerned about false alarms and a delayed reopening.

This decision traps the night inside a technical problem-solving mindset when it should have become an evacuation.

## 10:18 PM — Luis discovers the pattern

Using CCTV timestamps, Luis recognizes that The Attendant's route repeatedly corresponds to the most recent accountability page.

He begins deliberately triggering sound and door events to redirect it.

## 10:27 PM — North service incident

A shutter closes unexpectedly. Andre is injured but reaches the loading area.

Jo and remaining staff evacuate him.

## 10:36 PM — Luis remains behind

Luis stays to monitor Eli and keep the service route open. Jo leaves only after believing Luis has another exit.

She never sees him again.

## 10:43 PM — Eli reaches below grade

Eli determines that power alone is not the problem. The closing hold is tied to the staff-accountability state.

His job recorder begins documenting names and terminal messages.

## 10:51 PM — Luis kills the Security PA feed

The system continues speaking.

Luis records his final VHS/security note: the voice and the thing in the corridor are separate.

## 11:03 PM — Luis is trapped

Luis barricades the secondary security room. The final camera feed shows The Attendant waiting outside after a staff call.

The tape ends without showing his death.

## 11:14 PM — Eli finds the unresolved contractor session

Eli sees his own temporary contractor session stuck inside the closing state.

He attempts to clear it but discovers PCAS will not accept an ordinary contractor checkout while a wider accountability fault exists.

## 11:26 PM — Eli heads for PA/records control

He learns enough to suspect that the shift itself must be ended in the correct sequence rather than simply depowering the system.

## 11:38 PM — Eli is injured

Eli encounters The Attendant in the east service/records route. He escapes the immediate encounter but is badly hurt.

## 11:47 PM — Eli's final recording

Eli records that the building is “still counting” and that killing power only causes the closing process to resume when emergency circuits return.

He tries to reach a maintenance bypass adjacent to records control.

## Before midnight — Eli dies

Eli dies in a sealed service cavity/utility passage near the route to PA control.

His badge remains active.

PCAS never receives a valid exit event.

## After midnight — Pinewood fails to close

The official shift changes date.

PCAS remains in closing hold.

The unresolved work order and contractor session become persistent data.

Pinewood begins repeating the closing routine every night.

# 7. Present-day protagonist

The player character remains intentionally unnamed.

They are an ordinary outside maintenance contractor dispatched to diagnose an emergency electrical condition at a closed property.

The player should not have a secret personal connection to Pinewood. The horror is stronger if this could have happened to almost anyone who accepted the ticket.

The player's practical competence explains why they can operate breakers, relays, key systems and facility controls.

The player's lack of special knowledge preserves discovery.

PCAS identifies the player as:

> **CONTRACTOR 14**

Early in the game this sounds like an impersonal dispatch identifier.

Later, Renee discovers Contractor 01 through Contractor 13 in legacy records.

That is the reveal that Pinewood has been trying this for years.

---

# 8. Renee Ward — present-day Dispatcher

Renee is the primary living character.

She must become someone the player is relieved to hear from.

## Personality

Renee is competent, dry, practical and initially slightly impatient with Pinewood's terrible records. She does not immediately believe supernatural explanations, but she never treats the player like an idiot.

When evidence becomes impossible, she changes her mind because the evidence changes, not because the genre demands it.

## Arc

### Chapter 1

Renee handles the call as routine after-hours dispatch. She checks circuit status, warns about liability and tells the player to keep the radio open.

As power returns she sees data coming from cameras that should be decommissioned.

Her tone shifts from administrative to concerned.

### Chapter 2

Renee finds the original work-order creation date: 1997.

She starts searching archived property records while the player works below grade.

She explicitly tells the player she is staying on the line.

### Chapter 3

Renee finds names from the 1997 incident and begins reconstructing the old shift.

The player and Renee establish a radio-authentication habit after interference begins. At first, real Renee always opens important instructions with a recognizable phrase such as:

> “Fourteen, Ward on dispatch.”

This is a character habit, not a magical password.

### Chapter 4

Pinewood begins imitating Renee.

The first false instruction is subtle and useful enough that the player may obey it.

Then real Renee returns and says she has not transmitted for several minutes.

Later, the imitation uses her authentication phrase too.

This tells both characters that Pinewood is listening and learning.

From that point, Renee avoids giving blind route instructions. She gives verifiable information instead.

### Chapter 5

Renee uncovers Jo Alvarez's suppressed incident statement and Martin Kessler's override records.

She becomes the player's research partner, cross-referencing names while the player finds physical evidence.

### Chapter 6

Renee remains present through the beginning of the final closing sequence, but the signal becomes unreliable as Pinewood overlays its remembered 1997 state.

Near the final accountability step, the radio becomes useless because Renee and the imitation overlap.

The player must finish based on what they have learned rather than wait for instructions.

Renee's real voice returns only after the player reaches the exterior.

# 9. The Attendant — narrative rules

## What it is not

- It is not Eli Mercer.
- It is not a former security guard in a costume.
- It is not the PA voice.
- It is not a boss to be defeated through combat.
- It should never deliver a lore monologue.

## What the player can learn

The Attendant appears most reliably after Pinewood issues an accountability/staff-location call.

It behaves as though it is responding to a request to locate, return or account for staff.

It follows physical routes rather than teleporting through walls during ordinary gameplay.

Sound can redirect it because it treats activity as evidence of a worker's location.

It becomes more purposeful as the closing hold escalates.

## The older mystery

A pre-PCAS janitorial log from the 1980s refers to “that night employee again” in a corridor after closing.

A faded security photograph may contain an indistinct dark figure whose proportions resemble The Attendant.

Neither item proves identity.

The intended implication is:

> PCAS may not have invented The Attendant. It may simply have given something already associated with Pinewood a formal task, repeated thousands of times.

This should never be resolved further.

# 10. Six-chapter campaign

Target first-playthrough length: approximately **5–7 hours**, longer for thorough exploration.

Each chapter must differ in environment, dominant mechanic, story purpose and emotional rhythm.

---

## Chapter 1 — CLOSING TIME

### Environment

Public Pinewood Mall:

- central concourse and dry fountain,
- Sunburst Arcade,
- Video Planet,
- Pinewood Food Court,
- Cassette Castle,
- dark storefronts,
- public restrooms,
- customer-service remnants,
- freight elevator/service threshold.

### Visual identity

The most recognizable “dead mall” chapter.

Dirty terrazzo, faded neon, half-lit storefronts, abandoned promotional material and distant mall music. Most spaces are still architecturally understandable.

### Gameplay identity

Exploration and introductory stealth.

Restore circuits A/B/C, but each circuit meaningfully changes the environment:

- powers a section of lighting,
- activates or reveals a route,
- changes signage/PA behavior,
- awakens more of the closing system.

The player learns hiding, sound, sprint risk and decoys.

### Story beats

1. Routine arrival and radio check with Renee.
2. Pinewood is supposed to have been empty for six years.
3. The first circuit returns power.
4. A decommissioned camera produces movement data.
5. PA announces that closing has been extended and staff must remain.
6. First distant sighting of The Attendant should be staged before the first full hunt.
7. The PA addresses “Contractor Fourteen.”
8. Player can find the first three pieces of Last Shift evidence.
9. Master Service Key is recovered.
10. Renee tells the player to use the freight elevator and get out.
11. Elevator doors close.
12. The indicator counts **down**.

### Emotional endpoint

The player realizes this is not a haunted place they can simply leave by returning the way they came.

---

## Chapter 2 — BELOW GRADE

### Environment

Pinewood's service level:

- electrical rooms,
- loading corridors,
- boiler/HVAC spaces,
- pump room,
- utility tunnels,
- partially flooded low areas,
- freight receiving,
- abandoned maintenance cage.

### Visual identity

Industrial, claustrophobic and acoustically harsh.

Concrete, pipes, conduit, machinery, emergency lamps, standing water and metal service doors. Little commercial color remains.

### Gameplay identity

**Noise management and machinery.**

The player must restore two service relays and reroute power through loud equipment.

Machinery creates a tradeoff:

- loud equipment can mask the player's footsteps,
- but it also masks The Attendant's approach,
- some operations create temporary high-noise events that draw The Attendant.

This chapter should make sound itself feel unreliable.

### Major set piece — Gavin Cole

The player finds the body of **Gavin Cole**, a property inspector missing since 2023.

His phone/clipboard contains a Pinewood service ticket with language eerily similar to the player's current job.

This is the first undeniable proof that the loop is contemporary.

### Story beats

1. Renee confirms the elevator was never supposed to descend there.
2. Player restores service relay D.
3. Renee finds the work order's original creation date: November 14, 1997.
4. Player finds Gavin.
5. Gavin's evidence references “Contractor 13.”
6. The concept of earlier contractor sessions appears.
7. Relay E restores access toward Security/north service routes.
8. Renee finds references to the original closing manager and security supervisor.

### Emotional endpoint

The player understands that Pinewood did not merely wake up tonight. **It has been calling people back for years.**

---

## Chapter 3 — EYES IN SECURITY

### Environment

Security and receiving complex:

- primary security office,
- secondary security room,
- CCTV wall,
- key-control office,
- loading dock overlooks,
- evidence/storage cages,
- staff corridors,
- shutter controls.

### Visual identity

Institutional surveillance horror.

CRT monitors, timestamp overlays, dead camera feeds, fluorescent offices, mirrored observation glass and locked evidence spaces.

### Gameplay identity

**CCTV route planning.**

The player can restore camera groups and use a security console to:

- view parts of the map remotely,
- track The Attendant imperfectly,
- open one controlled shutter while closing another,
- create route opportunities.

Cameras are useful but not infallible.

Occasionally a camera shows The Attendant somewhere it is not physically present. Occasionally a feed shows an empty corridor seconds before the real creature enters.

The system should never randomly cheat during a lethal moment. False imagery is a narrative pressure, not unfair AI teleportation.

### Major set piece — Luis Ortega

The player opens secondary Security and finds Luis Ortega's remains behind an old barricade.

Nearby is his final security VHS/evidence.

Luis's recording establishes the crucial pattern:

> Staff page first. The Attendant second.

He also physically disconnected announcement equipment before the PA continued.

### Story beats

1. Renee identifies Luis from a 1997 staff record.
2. CCTV footage lets the player observe The Attendant without immediate danger.
3. Luis's remains validate the old incident.
4. Security evidence shows the PA and Attendant are separate phenomena.
5. Renee and player establish a recognizable radio-authentication habit as interference worsens.
6. The player unlocks the route toward Pinewood's sealed east wing.

### Emotional endpoint

The monster has a rule. That is comforting for about thirty seconds, until the player realizes the building is the thing issuing the rule.

---

## Chapter 4 — THE EAST WING

### Environment

A long-sealed retail/staff zone:

- abandoned anchor-store interior,
- employee lockers,
- training room,
- staff break room,
- tenant back corridors,
- shuttered boutiques,
- old receiving passage,
- partially collapsed decorative mall areas.

### Visual identity

Personal, nostalgic and increasingly impossible.

Unlike Chapter 2's industrial decay, this chapter contains human remnants: lockers, photographs, notes, employee schedules, break-room decorations and 1997 retail clutter.

Occasional lighting or signage appears briefly too clean or too current for the ruined mall.

### Gameplay identity

**Information distrust.**

Pinewood begins imitating Renee over the radio.

The player cannot simply follow spoken directions. They must cross-check:

- physical maps,
- room numbers,
- power states,
- objective evidence,
- known radio behavior.

The game should never require the player to identify voice acting by tiny audio differences. False instructions must be disprovable through world information.

### Tessa and Jo become real people

The player finds:

- Tessa's Cassette Castle locker contents,
- a staff photo containing Tessa, Jo, Luis and others,
- Tessa's personal mixtape,
- Jo's handwritten closing notes,
- evidence that Tessa survived the night.

The 1997 incident stops feeling like an abstract ghost story and becomes a workplace disaster with identifiable people.

### Radio twist

The player receives a plausible instruction from “Renee.”

Later the real Renee returns:

> She has not transmitted since before that instruction.

Soon after, the false voice learns Renee's authentication phrase.

From here on, even a familiar voice is not proof.

### Emotional endpoint

The player's last reliable connection to the outside world has become a medium Pinewood can imitate.

---

## Chapter 5 — ACCOUNTABILITY

### Environment

Records and management infrastructure:

- mall administration offices,
- payroll/archive room,
- lease records,
- operations director office,
- old computer room,
- time-clock station,
- PA service plant,
- sealed utility passages.

### Visual identity

Paper, dust and bureaucracy.

File boxes, dot-matrix printouts, employee cards, shift calendars, punch records, binders, fax paper and dying terminals. This chapter should feel quieter and more investigative than the others.

### Gameplay identity

**Reconstruction.**

Instead of restoring another set of breakers, the player reconstructs the final shift:

- who was scheduled,
- who actually left,
- which records were falsified,
- which account remained active,
- what closing sequence PCAS expected.

The Attendant spends more time stalking than openly hunting, producing long stretches of uncomfortable quiet while the player reads rooms and solves evidence relationships.

### Management cover-up

Renee uncovers Jo Alvarez's suppressed statement while the player finds Martin Kessler's override documentation.

The player learns Kessler authorized inaccurate “all clear” records to reopen Pinewood.

There is no occult conspiracy. Human cowardice and liability management allowed the problem to persist.

### Major set piece — Eli Mercer

The player opens a sealed service passage and finds Eli's remains.

His contractor badge identifies him before Renee confirms the name.

His final recorder proves:

- he understood the closing process could not simply be depowered,
- he attempted to reach PA control,
- he died before his contractor session cleared.

This is where the game explicitly disproves the tempting theory that The Attendant is Eli's ghost.

The Attendant can be heard elsewhere while Eli's body is physically in front of the player.

### Major reveal

Renee discovers that the current “Contractor Fourteen” field is the fourteenth session tied to the same 1997 maintenance request.

The player is part of the same accounting structure now.

### Emotional endpoint

The player finally knows what happened.

Now they must willingly perform the closing process that killed the people who first tried to understand it.

## Chapter 6 — THE LAST SHIFT

### Environment

A transformed route through Pinewood's closing memory, combining new PA/control spaces with selected remixed areas from earlier chapters.

### Visual identity

**1997 bleeding through 2026.**

The ruined mall intermittently overlays its remembered closing-night state:

- clean tile replaces grime for seconds at a time,
- the dry fountain runs,
- Cassette Castle lights up,
- an arcade attract screen glows,
- sale signs show November 1997,
- staff silhouettes perform fragments of closing tasks,
- then a ballast buzz or power flicker snaps everything back to ruin.

This is not a random dreamscape. Every transformation should correspond to PCAS reconstructing part of its closing routine.

### Gameplay identity

**Perform the final closing sequence while being hunted by the system enforcing it.**

The player must complete a multi-stage ritual grounded in mundane procedure:

1. verify final staff roster,
2. close/acknowledge selected zones,
3. process unresolved key/service states,
4. reach PA/accountability control,
5. account for the 1997 staff,
6. clear Eli Mercer's contractor session.

The Attendant's search behavior becomes tied to active staff calls. When PCAS calls a name/location, The Attendant heads toward the place that employee should be.

The player can use this predictably to create routes, turning learned lore into gameplay mastery.

### The final twist

The player clears Eli Mercer.

The terminal updates.

> **ACCOUNTABILITY: 1**

The remaining active session is:

> **CONTRACTOR 14**

The player realizes that entering Pinewood registered them as current staff for the unresolved shift.

They cannot end the shift while they are still inside and active.

The final objective becomes physically clocking out/clearing the contractor session and reaching the employee exit while Pinewood attempts one final staff recall.

### Final chase

This chase should be earned rather than merely faster AI.

The route passes through brief 1997/present overlays while PCAS repeatedly calls:

> “Contractor Fourteen, return to your assigned station.”

The Attendant responds.

The player must use everything learned about routes, sound, calls and closing states to reach the exit.

# 16. PCAS behavior and language

PCAS should sound like institutional software, not a demonic computer.

Preferred language:

- ACCOUNTABILITY HOLD
- STAFF STATUS UNRESOLVED
- CLOSING CHECKLIST INCOMPLETE
- RETURN TO ASSIGNED STATION
- KEY CONTROL DISCREPANCY
- CONTRACTOR SESSION ACTIVE
- SECURITY STATE PENDING
- ALL STAFF ACCOUNTED FOR

Avoid:

- overt threats,
- evil laughter,
- “I see you,”
- supernatural villain monologues,
- referring to itself as a conscious being.

The terror comes from calm procedural language continuing under impossible circumstances.

# 17. Pinewood voice imitation rules

Pinewood can reproduce speech heard through its systems, but the imitation develops over time.

## Early

- clipped phrases,
- old PA cadence,
- wrong context,
- no convincing back-and-forth.

## Middle

- can reproduce a full Renee-like instruction,
- may use stale information,
- occasionally mixes modern phrasing with old 1997 terminology.

## Late

- can imitate Renee convincingly enough that voice alone is useless,
- can repeat the authentication phrase after hearing it,
- cannot reliably invent facts it has never observed or accessed.

This is why Renee shifts from “follow my directions” to “verify everything I tell you against something physical.”

# 18. Endings

## Standard ending — PINEWOOD IS CLOSED

Requirements:

- complete mandatory Chapter 6 evidence,
- clear enough accountability fields to force a closing release,
- do not possess/reconstruct the full LS-01 through LS-09 evidence set.

The player performs an incomplete reconciliation and clears their own session long enough to escape.

PCAS announces that Pinewood is closed.

The Attendant stops moving.

At dawn Renee confirms that the active work ticket has closed.

Later that night, Renee's dispatch console receives another Pinewood emergency request.

The new field reads:

> **CONTRACTOR 15**

The player escaped, but the unresolved historical logic remains capable of rebuilding the loop.

## True ending — EVERYONE CLOCKED OUT

Requirements:

- LS-01 through LS-09 recovered/reconstructed,
- full final-shift roster available,
- correct account statuses entered,
- Eli Mercer explicitly cleared,
- player clears Contractor 14 and physically exits.

PCAS reaches:

> **ACCOUNTABILITY: 0**

The final closing announcement begins normally.

It stops before repeating.

Lights turn off section by section behind the player.

The Attendant stands at the far end of the concourse.

Its white eyes extinguish.

The employee exit releases.

Outside, Renee's radio returns clearly.

Her system shows the Pinewood ticket not merely closed but archived with an impossible completion reference tied back to the original 1997 work order.

No Contractor 15 ticket appears.

The player leaves at dawn.

## What the true ending does not answer

It does not explain where The Attendant came from.

It proves only that once Pinewood genuinely has no one left to account for, the building stops calling.

And when Pinewood stops calling, nothing answers.

# 20. Narrative fairness rules

1. Mandatory objectives cannot depend on noticing tiny optional text.
2. The player is never killed for trusting a fake radio line with no available way to verify it.
3. Major twists must be foreshadowed before they are explained.
4. A collectible can deepen a reveal, but critical plot comprehension must survive missing it unless that collectible explicitly belongs to the true-ending path.
5. Do not use random architectural changes as a substitute for level design.
6. The Attendant's ordinary navigation remains physically coherent.
7. Supernatural effects may distort perception and electronics without invalidating established gameplay rules at arbitrary moments.
8. Named characters must have at least one humanizing detail unrelated to their death or the supernatural plot.
9. The game should periodically provide quiet spaces where the player can process evidence.
10. Every chapter ends with a meaningful change in the player's understanding, not merely a door opening.

---

# 21. Dialogue rules

## Renee

Natural, concise, technically competent. She can joke lightly early but should not become a quip machine.

She should react to discoveries as a person processing information in real time rather than as a narrator reading lore.

## PCAS / Intercom

Formal, repetitive, calm and procedural.

Its lines become frightening because context changes, not because the vocabulary becomes demonic.

## Historical recordings

People speak like workers under stress, not horror writers.

They should use incomplete observations:

- “I saw...”
- “The camera says...”
- “Jo already checked that door...”
- “That shouldn't have power...”

Avoid characters conveniently explaining the entire mythology into a tape recorder.
